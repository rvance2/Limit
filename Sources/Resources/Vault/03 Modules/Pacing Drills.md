# Pacing Drills

Ten minutes, once or twice weekly. Least fashionable skill module, one of the more useful.

## What the research points at

Two findings, both counterintuitive:

- More experienced climbers spend a *greater* percentage of time immobile. Read as co-adaptation — stillness preserves stability, particularly when keeping the centre of mass close to the wall.
- Jerk of the hip trajectory is lower in more fluent climbers, and total jerk decreases with experience.

Together: good pacing is neither uniformly fast nor slow. Smooth, with deliberate stillness where stillness buys position.

## The drills

**Three tempos.** A boulder I've done, climbed three times: fast as possible, slow as possible, natural. Three minutes rest between.

Slow teaches which positions are actually stable and which I've been rushing past because they aren't. Fast teaches which holds I don't need to fully engage. The comparison is the point.

**Tempo rehearsal.** Before a project attempt, count the sequence out loud with the intended timing. "One — two-three — four." Then match the count on the wall. Trains timing separately from movement, which is how musicians learn hard passages.

**One-move-one-breath.** Exactly one breath per move. Forces rhythm, exposes where I flail.

**Deceleration audit.** Film an attempt. Find every place I decelerated mid-move. Nearly always wasted, and usually a commitment problem rather than a strength problem. [[Commitment]].

## The two errors to look for

**Rushing the setup.** Arriving at the crux with hips slightly wrong because I moved through the previous move too fast. Check this before concluding anything about strength.

**Hesitating at the commitment point.** Pausing where holding the position costs more than the move would. On a bad crimp, deliberation is the most expensive option.
