# Recruitment Pulls

Overcoming isometrics. Pull as hard and as fast as possible against something that doesn't move, and measure every rep.

## Dose

- 5-8 efforts, 3-5 s each.
- Maximum intent from the first millisecond. Not a ramp.
- 2-3 min rest.
- Half crimp on 20 mm for primary sets. Nelson's note: for connective tissue emphasis, move to 25-30 mm afterwards, since larger edges up to ~30 mm permit higher peak vertical force.
- Twice weekly.

## Setup

With a load cell (Tindeq or similar): edge anchored to floor via the device, pull upward from a stable position, watch peak live. This is the version that works. The live readout isn't a nicety — the campus board researchers used real-time biofeedback on a screen specifically to guarantee maximal effort from trained athletes, which says how much they trusted verbal instruction.

Without one: stand under a fixed edge with an immovable anchor, or a barbell-and-block arrangement heavier than I can lift. No measurement, so substitute a proxy — fixed setup, fixed position, subjective 1-10 per rep, hard stop after three consecutive ratings below the session best.

## Progression

Not by load. By measured peak force. Track the session best weekly. Noisy curve, slow trend.

## Stop rule, unusual

Stop when peak drops more than 5% below the session best. Two more reps past that trains fatigue tolerance, not recruitment. Some sessions that's rep four. Take it.

## Why both this and hangs

Overcoming and yielding. Nelson's position is that overcoming improves force output that shows up in climbing while heavy hangs bias connective tissue stiffness, with ~30% difference between the two measurements. No randomised trial in climbers comparing them on performance, so I run both. [[Max Hangs]] handles yielding.
