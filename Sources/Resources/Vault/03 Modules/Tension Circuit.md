# Tension Circuit

Fifteen minutes, twice weekly, end of session. One of the few things here that can go last, because nothing in it is neurally demanding.

## What the research supports

Saeterbakken et al. (2018), n=19, ten weeks, twice weekly, isometric vs dynamic core training. Neither beat the other. Isometric: +10.8% trunk flexion MVC, +29.6% body-lift climbing test. Dynamic: +5.0-14.9% across core strength tests including both rotation directions, +33.8% body-lift though not significantly at n=9 per group.

Authors' recommendation, which I follow: dynamic for oblique strength, isometric for transfer. So the circuit does both.

Their baseline finding is worth holding: trunk flexion MVC in these climbers was 94-99% of bodyweight, 30-50% above an age-matched non-climbing population. I already have a strong core. Expect small gains.

## The circuit

Twice through, 45 s work, 20 s transition.

**Dynamic half**
1. Foot-lift on a steep wall — hang from two holds, lift knee or foot to a foot stance, alternate. From the study's protocol.
2. Arm lock-off reaches — one arm and same-side foot on the wall, free arm reaches horizontally as far as possible for a hold, return. Alternate. Also from the study, and it produces high oblique EMG.
3. Hanging leg raises, controlled, no swing.

**Isometric half**
4. Body-lift hold — top position of 1, held.
5. Side plank, top arm overhead, 30 s each side.
6. Front lever progression at my level: tuck, advanced tuck, or one-leg.

## Progression

Steeper wall, smaller base of support, longer reach, or a harder lever tuck. Not more time past 45 s.

## Shoulder note

Exercise 2 puts the supporting shoulder in a loaded reach. Keep the scapula set. If it drifts into a shrugged, protracted position the exercise is too hard and the fix is a shorter reach. [[Shoulder Protocol]].

## Also

The best tension training is steep climbing with bad feet. This supplements, doesn't replace. Given a choice between the circuit and 20 minutes on the 40° wall, take the wall.
