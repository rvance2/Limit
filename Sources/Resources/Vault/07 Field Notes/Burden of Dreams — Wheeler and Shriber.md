# Burden of Dreams — Wheeler and Shriber

Coaching breakdown between an American boulderer and his coach Charlie Shriber, covering two training cycles for Burden of Dreams, a pulley rupture between them, and the tactical work done on site.

## What this is worth

Tier 3. One athlete, one coach, one V17, auto-transcribed so garbled in places, and it contains a plug for the coach's own programme. None of it is evidence in the sense [[Sources]] uses the word.

It's here anyway because it's dense with *mechanistic technique detail* — the specific "push here, flex this, move your body down as you reach" material that the peer-reviewed literature contains almost none of. Cross-sectional studies tell me elite climbers produce force faster. They do not tell me how to keep a foot on.

Treat everything below as a hypothesis to test in a session, not a finding.

## Ideas I took

### The shotgun simulation strategy

Rather than building one replica of the project, set **four or five simulators that each approximate the boulder in a slightly different way**. Train across all of them.

Three reasons given, all of which hold:

1. Beta uncertainty. Arriving prepared for every version of the crux rather than the one version guessed at home.
2. It doesn't pigeonhole. Training five adjacent problems still makes you better generally; training one replica makes you better at one replica.
3. It doesn't get boring, so adherence survives.

Directly relevant to me: no systems board, no replica capability. This is the workaround. → [[Simulation Sets]]

### Technical focal points in the warm-up

The coach wrote ~10 on-the-wall warm-up drills, each with a single technical focal point, aimed at the nervous system rather than at temperature.

Example: stay on two hands, leave the feet on tiny holds, and move the toes around — shift weight, roll the big toe, feel pressure change direction, without moving the hands.

Slab version: spend a fixed time on a foothold. Can't move normally. Stop, stay, and feel.

Coach's framing: establish dependency on something new, increase frequency of attention. "Today's warm-up, I want your attention on this aspect of movement." The point is to break the autopilot of *I warm up like this*, and replace it with awareness of one body part.

The athlete's claim is that these drills, not the strength work, are what made the drop-knee on Burden feasible. → [[Focal Point Warm-Up]]

### Micro-beta as attention management

The best mental idea in the transcript.

> "For the first move and for the cross, I had enough micro beta to where I could focus on the micro beta mentally in order to distract from the macro idea of sending the boulder. It takes you out of the flow if you're thinking about the implications of your send. So you always want some directive for every move. And for the last move, I just didn't really have enough directive."

So the function of a movement cue on a redpoint burn is not primarily to improve the movement. It's to **occupy the attention that would otherwise go to the outcome**. A move without a directive is a hole where outcome-thinking gets in.

He also quantified it, which is honest: the foot-push cue was maybe 5% of doing the move physically, and 15% of directing his mind so he could do it in link. → [[Focus]]

### Active foot, and pushing down rather than up

Two related mechanics, both specific.

**The foot is a force producer, not a placeholder.** On a sloped foothold with the heel down, most climbers leave the foot passive during the move. Actively plantarflexing — driving the toe down, engaging the calf — does two things: it exerts downward force which offloads the hands *before* the move starts, and it locks a weight-bearing joint, which adds stability. Fingers supporting less weight means the hands can move faster.

**Drop the body as you reach.** Counterintuitive and the part that made the athlete say "I've never not tried to go up towards a hold."

> "When you push down, naturally your body goes up. So as I reach, instead of staying neutral or going up, I actually drop my body down. So now I'm pushing into the foot, which would push me up, but I'm moving my bodyweight at the foot. So it forces more weight into the foothold."

Changing the objective from *get the hand up* to *get the hand while driving weight into the foot*. → [[Footwork]], [[Leverage]]

### Buy time by moving up for longer

On a hard throw, going lower before initiating did two things. Better angle of pull on the hands, which is obvious. And a longer upward trajectory, which is not:

> "As he's moving up, he's grabbing and establishing contractile force before he starts coming down. So he had more time to develop contact strength and also get the hand there."

Reframes a dyno as a time-purchase problem rather than a power problem. More time in the ascending phase means more milliseconds to close the hand on the hold before gravity reverses. That is directly the ~200 ms window in [[Contact Strength]]. → [[Contact Strength]], [[Deadpoint Power]]

### One tick mark, not two

He was using a goalpost tick — one mark for the index edge, one for the pinky edge — as everyone does, and landing consistently two inches off on a hold with no margin.

Coach: aiming at one thing is more accurate than aiming at two.

Accuracy stopped being an issue that session. Free, obvious in hindsight, and he says it never entered his field of view because there was too much else to think about. → [[Precision]]

### The ceiling pusher

Coach's framing on why to keep training a strength you already have while training a weakness:

> "You never want to lose your ceiling pusher."

Model: a boulder demands a combined score. If it asks for V16 finger strength plus V14-15 technical awareness, that's ~30 points. Being V18 strong means you need less technique because you can overpower the moves. So the strength you're a super-adapter in is doing load-bearing work and shouldn't be dropped to zero while you train weaknesses — one to two sessions a week keeps it.

Crude model, no data behind it, and useful anyway as an allocation heuristic. → [[Factors MOC]]

### Curate, don't overhaul

For a specific objective in your existing style, the plan was not a new training system. It was the same training — boards — with the *selection* of boulders narrowed to those resembling the goal, plus a small number of targeted additions for the elements that didn't suit him.

The first question the coach asked was "what have you been doing lately that's working?"

### Holds warm up when you rapid-fire

Offhand comment during the session: a hold that gets attempted repeatedly stays warm and greasy.

Independent second reason for the rest intervals in [[Attempt Discipline]], which currently justifies them on phosphocreatine grounds alone. → [[Friction]]

### Pulley rehab, structurally

He ruptured a pulley between trips, worked with a PT, and returned stronger. The structure worth keeping:

- Kept training load elsewhere immediately. Slab, feet-only, toe work, one-arm climbing for coordination, and continued lifting. The goal was not to lose fitness.
- **Graded progressive overload on the injured hand:** 15-second pulls on a 20 mm edge, starting around 25 lb and progressing to ~90 lb across a month, with the target being parity with the uninjured hand.
- Pulley ring for support once climbing resumed, months two and three.
- Came back feeling *better* on boards, attributing it to the uninjured fingers finally getting rest.

**The one thing not to copy:** he climbed two days after the rupture, on a peer's advice. That was done with professional oversight, on an elite athlete, against a competition deadline. It is not general advice and I should not read it as permission. → [[Tendon Health]]

## Ideas I didn't take

- **Grade-point arithmetic as a planning tool.** Fine as a mental model, useless as a calculation. Nobody knows their technical grade to a number.
- **Anything about replica building.** No board, and the shotgun approach is the better answer for me anyway.
