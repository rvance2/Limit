# Shoes

No research worth citing. Enormous practical effect. Mechanics and consensus.

## Fit before model

A badly fitting aggressive shoe performs worse than a well-fitting moderate one. What matters for limit bouldering:

- **Toe contact.** Big toe reaches the end, no dead space. Dead space means the shoe deforms before force reaches the rock — the largest single edging loss.
- **Heel volume.** Loose heel means heel hooks slip and the heel rolls in the cup. For a project with a decisive heel hook, heel fit outranks toe fit.
- **Midsole stiffness.** Stiff holds small edges with less foot strength and gives less feedback. Soft conforms to volumes and smears and demands more foot strength.

## Downturn and asymmetry

Downturn tensions the toe and pulls me toward the wall on steep ground. Costs on slabs and on flat edges. Asymmetry concentrates force on the big toe — helps on small holds, hurts on smears.

Pick for the crux, not the boulder. If the crux is one small edge, take the stiffest, most asymmetric shoe I own even if the rest is slabby.

## Retirement

Two failure points, one obvious.

Obvious: rubber thickness at the toe. Midsole visible → edging gone.

Non-obvious: upper stretch. A stretched upper no longer transmits force even with good rubber, because my foot moves inside it. Happens before the rubber wears out on most shoes, and it's why a resoled shoe "never feels the same." It was the upper.

Keep a fresh pair for project days. Not broken-in. Fresh.

## Cold

Rubber stiffens when cold and loses conformity. On a cold project day keep the shoes inside my jacket between attempts and put them on warm. Real effect, nearly free. [[Temperature]].
