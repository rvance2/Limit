# Friction

Two separate systems with different physics that I discuss as one thing: skin-rock and rubber-rock.

## Skin-rock

Not a fixed material property. Depends on water content of the stratum corneum, which changes the tribological and mechanical properties of the skin surface. Too dry and the skin is hard and won't conform to the asperities. Too wet and a fluid layer forms. Narrow optimum in between.

Which is why the same crimp feels different at 8am and 2pm with nothing else changed.

## What chalk does, and it isn't what I think

One study on the finger-hold friction coefficient found chalk *decreased* it, and that chalk doesn't dry the hands the way climbers assume. Clarke et al. (2026) tested chalk on sandstone, granite, dark and light limestone across varying roughness and found its effectiveness depends on moisture level and on the gradient of the asperities at the rock surface.

Reconciliation: chalk is moisture management, not a friction enhancer. On dry skin on smooth rock it can reduce grip. On sweaty skin it removes the fluid layer and helps a great deal. So the answer to "more chalk?" is "how wet are my hands?"

- Dry hands, smooth rock → less chalk. Brushing the hold matters more.
- Sweating → liquid chalk before, loose chalk during. Liquid handles excess moisture and oils better; in humid conditions loose chalk gets absorbed by hand moisture too fast to do much.
- Brush the hold. Old chalk on a hold is a lubricant. Highest-return, least-done friction intervention at any crag.

## Holds warm up under rapid attempts

A hold that gets attempted repeatedly retains heat from skin contact and stays warm and greasy. Noted offhand in [[Burden of Dreams — Wheeler and Shriber]] during a session where a crux hold felt worse as attempts stacked up.

No measurement behind it, and the mechanism is obvious enough that I believe it. It gives [[Attempt Discipline]]'s rest interval a second justification independent of phosphocreatine, and it argues for brushing on a schedule rather than by feel.

## Rubber-rock

Different physics. Dominated by viscoelastic hysteresis and real contact area, which is why softer compounds grip better on smooth rock and deform into asperities. [[Rubber]].

Rubber is also temperature sensitive. Cold rubber is stiffer and less conforming. Real winter trade-off: my skin wants cold and my rubber doesn't. Warming shoes before a hard attempt isn't superstition.
