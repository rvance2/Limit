# Temperature

I treat temperature as the master variable. The research says humidity matters more.

## The data

Finger flexor literature comparing roughly 50°F to 80°F (10°C to 27°C):

| Measure | Warmer |
| --- | --- |
| Maximum force production | No difference |
| Endurance | Lower |
| Recovery after fatigue | Faster |

Read that carefully. Max force doesn't change across that range. Endurance drops when warm, but recovery is *faster* when warm. So for a four-move boulder with five-minute rests, moderate heat is much less costly than I assume, and might help between-attempt recovery.

Proposed mechanism for the endurance drop: competition for blood flow between skin vasodilation and forearm muscle perfusion.

## Where the real effect lives

Not my muscles. My skin. Cold skin is harder, sweats less, conforms differently. Anecdotal ranges cluster around 0-5°C for optimal skin grip. That's [[Friction]], not physiology.

And my [[Rubber]], which stiffens in cold and loses conformity.

## Rock temperature vs air temperature

Rock temperature is what matters and it lags air by hours. Sun-baked sandstone stays warm long after sunset. A north-facing boulder in all-day shade can be 8°C below the air reading on my phone. Buy an infrared thermometer — cheap, and it settles arguments.

Rock must also be above the dew point or moisture condenses on it. [[Humidity]].

## Precooling

Research on heat-stress fatigue supports it. One litre at ~4°C before or during the session, or a slushy, which works better because of the phase change. [[Hydration]].

## Tactical summary

- Skin and rubber: colder is better, down to where my fingers stop working and the rubber goes glassy. Floor around 0 to −2°C.
- Forearms: temperature barely matters for a single max effort.
- Long session: cooler helps endurance, warmer helps between-attempt recovery. On a limit boulder with long rests that's closer to a wash than folklore suggests.
- Track rock temperature, not air temperature.
