# Sleep

Largest recovery lever, best evidence, none of it in climbers.

## The data

Mah et al. (2011), collegiate basketball, n=11. Baseline sleep, then extension to a target of ten hours in bed. Objective nightly sleep increased 110.9 ± 79.7 min. After extension:

- Timed sprint 16.2 ± 0.61 s → 15.5 ± 0.54 s.
- Free throw percentage +9%; three-point percentage +9.2%.
- Mean PVT reaction time decreased; Epworth Sleepiness Scale scores fell.
- POMS improved — vigour up, fatigue down.

Small n, one sport, no separate control group. Sleep extension studies in swimmers and others point the same way, and a systematic review of sleep extension in athletes supports the direction. Extensions of roughly 45-120 min per night appear to matter. Recommendations for athletes in heavy training land at 8-10 hours.

## Why the accuracy findings are the interesting ones

Reaction time and precision matter more to me than sprint speed. The shooting-accuracy results are the closest analogue in that dataset to what I need — hitting a small target reliably under fatigue. Reaction time is the other, and the one most plausibly connected to catching a hold inside 200 ms. [[Contact Strength]] and [[Precision]].

Specific millisecond figures for Mah's reaction-time result circulate online. I couldn't verify them against the paper, so this note reports direction only.

## What to do

Dull and it works. [[Sleep Protocol]] has it. Short version:

- 8.5-9 h in bed on training days, 9+ before a project day.
- Fixed wake time. Consistency beats duration for the circadian part.
- The night *before* the night before matters. Debt on the eve is already priced in; two good nights beat one.
- Naps 20-30 min. Useful on double days and trips.
- Alcohol destroys sleep architecture. On a project trip that's a performance question, not a moral one.

## Sequencing

Sleep quality drops in the days after very heavy sessions, so the hardest weeks are also the weeks I sleep worst, and it compounds. Another reason the [[Deload]] weeks are load-bearing.
