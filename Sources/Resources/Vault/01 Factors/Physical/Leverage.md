# Leverage

The geometry deciding how much of my force reaches the hold in a useful direction. Least discussed physical factor, one of the more decisive.

No study here. This is mechanics, and mechanics doesn't need a p-value.

## Three geometries

**Distance from the wall.** My centre of mass hangs off the holds at some horizontal distance. That distance × bodyweight is the moment my fingers and shoulders resist. Pulling hips in ten centimetres on a steep wall reduces finger load measurably and costs nothing. Why [[Tension]] and hip position outperform raw strength on overhangs.

**Force vector against the hold.** A hold has a positive direction. Force 20° off it is partly spent trying to slide off. Foot position, hip rotation and shoulder position all rotate my vector. Most of what looks like "he's just stronger" on a bad hold is a better vector.

**Moment arms at the joints.** Longer segments, longer moment arms, more torque to resist for the same external load. The concrete cost of long limbs, and small relative to how much it gets invoked.

## Training it

Can't train geometry. Can train finding it — [[Beta]], [[Precision]], [[Movement Efficiency]] — and holding it once found, which is [[Tension]].

The drill that pays: on a move I can't do, don't add force. Change the vector. Rotate the hip, drop the shoulder, change which foot is loaded, move the foot two centimetres. Five vector experiments before concluding the move needs more strength. Most of the time it needed a different angle. [[Beta Lab]].

## Offloading before the move

The fastest way to make a hold feel better is to put less weight on it, and the fastest way to do that is the feet.

Actively driving a foot down before initiating a move transfers load off the hands *before* they have to move. Fingers supporting less weight can move faster, and the loaded joint is more stable. Counterintuitively that means moving bodyweight **down** into the foot as I reach up, rather than rising toward the hold. [[Footwork]] has the mechanics. Source: [[Burden of Dreams — Wheeler and Shriber]].

This is the same physics as the hip-distance point above, applied at the instant of the move rather than to the static position.

## Where leverage beats strength

Steep ground, bad feet, open-handed holds. On a vertical wall with good feet, strength dominates because geometry is nearly fixed. On a 40° overhang geometry is most of the game, which is also why steep boulders feel disproportionately easier as body tension improves.
