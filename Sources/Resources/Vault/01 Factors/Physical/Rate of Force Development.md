# Rate of Force Development

Peak force is what I could hold given time. RFD is what I can hold in the 100 ms after my hand lands. On a limit boulder those are frequently different problems.

## The data

Stien et al. (2021), n=57, isometric pull-up on 23 mm, 90° elbow. RFD over absolute windows (50, 100, 150, 200, 250 ms from onset) and relative windows (25, 50, 75, 95, 100% of time to peak).

From the abstract, the verifiable part:

- Elite higher than both other groups at **all relative time periods** (ES 1.02-1.58, p<0.001-0.002).
- On **absolute** windows, elite differed only at 50, 100 and 150 ms (ES 0.72-0.84, p=0.032-0.040).
- Intermediate vs advanced: no difference at any period (p=0.942-1.000).
- Authors recommend relative over absolute windows for climbers.

Bullets one and two are the actionable ones. Early-phase force is where the top separates; past ~150 ms the absolute differences wash out. A secondary summary gives ~1457 N/s elite against 799 and 801 at 50 ms — figure images, so indicative magnitudes only.

Caution on that source: the same write-up claims elite reached peak force faster while listing elite at 2065 ms and advanced at 1495 ms. Contradicts itself. I haven't used its time-to-peak claim anywhere.

**Stien et al. 2022**, n=78, same test, three IRCRA bands with cleaner separation. The paper that reweighted my plan:

| Measure | Intermediate → Advanced | Advanced → Elite |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Peak force | +40.1% (ES 1.24) | +39.7% (ES 1.40) |
| **RFD** | +45.5% (ES 1.42) | **+74.9%** (ES 1.42) |

Peak force climbs a flat ~40% per band. RFD nearly doubles into elite. The authors' reading: RFD becomes an increasingly important limiting factor once elite grades are reached, because those grades feature steeper terrain and longer moves demanding rapid rather than sustained force.

At V8-V10 I'm crossing that boundary, which is why [[Block 3 — Power and RFD]] went from four weeks to six. [[Factors MOC]].

Two caveats. Sport climbing — IRCRA 24-27 ≈ French 8a-8b+ — so bouldering transfer is assumed. And the paper reports the intermediate→advanced RFD comparison as p=0.046 in the abstract and p=0.057 in the discussion.

The campus board frequency trial (n=16, five weeks, volume-matched): four sessions/week improved RFD and absolute force; two sessions/week improved bouldering performance and moves-to-failure. Same total volume, opposite outcomes. Most useful practical finding in the climbing RFD literature — frequency for rate, concentration for performance. Sixteen people, five weeks. Don't over-read it.

## Training it without a campus board

No systems board, no rungs, so the standard tool is out. Substitutes, roughly by usefulness:

1. [[Recruitment Pulls]] — pull as hard and fast as possible against a fixed edge, 3-5 s, tracking peak and time-to-peak per rep. Trains the intent even if wall transfer is unproven.
2. [[Deadpoint Power]] — bounded dynamic moves where the catch is the hard part.
3. [[Contact Strength Ladder]] — progressive drop-and-catch, jugs then edges.
4. Hangboard pull-and-release: from two hands, unweight one, re-catch. Careful — [[Shoulder Protocol]].

## What kills it

Fatigue, and it kills RFD before it kills peak force. Any RFD work after limit bouldering is wasted. Any RFD work at 90% intent trains something else. Two truly maximal sets beat six nearly maximal ones.
