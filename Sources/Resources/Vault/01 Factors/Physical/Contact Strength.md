# Contact Strength

Catch a hold, generate enough force on it fast enough not to fall off. In practice [[Rate of Force Development]] plus the coordination to apply it at an unfamiliar angle, on skin that just landed at speed.

The time budget is brutal — estimates put the window under 200 ms. That's inside the range dominated by neural drive rather than cross-section, which is why the biggest climbers aren't always the best catchers.

## Evidence status

Thin. No clean intervention trial isolating contact strength in climbers. What exists:

- RFD in the first 50-150 ms discriminates elite from advanced.
- Campus board 4×/week improved RFD, n=16, five weeks.
- Everything else is coaching mechanism, mostly Power Company and CAMP4.

In the plan anyway: mechanism is sound, training is cheap, and the failure mode it addresses — hit the hold, pop off — is how most of my limit attempts end.

## Buying time instead of adding force

The 200 ms window is not fixed. It's a function of trajectory, which means it can be bought.

On a hard throw, dropping lower before initiating extends the upward phase of the movement. That means more milliseconds spent still rising while the hand is closing on the hold, before gravity reverses and the hold has to hold me:

> "As he's moving up, he's grabbing and establishing contractile force before he starts coming down. So he had more time to develop contact strength."

Reframes a dyno from a power problem into a time-purchase problem. Going lower also improves the angle of pull, which is the obvious half.

Practical: on any throw I'm consistently failing to stick, before adding force, try initiating from lower. Cheaper than getting stronger and available today. Source: [[Burden of Dreams — Wheeler and Shriber]], Tier 3.

## The spectrum

Drolet's framing. Progress along it, don't jump to the hard end.

1. Static hold, hand pre-placed. Baseline.
2. Slow re-grip. Place, adjust, load.
3. Bumps. Hold to hold at controlled speed.
4. Drop and catch on jugs.
5. Drop and catch on edges.
6. Deadpoints where the catch is the crux.
7. Full dynos.

I live at 3-6. Stage 7 is a shoulder liability and buys little.

## Stop rule

Any pop, click, or sting in a finger during catch work ends the exercise for the day. Not the set. The day. Pulley injuries happen in exactly this movement.
