# Recruitment

How much of the available motor-unit pool I can call on, and how fast. Why two climbers with the same forearm cross-section differ by two grades, and why a warm-up changes my max hang by 10%.

## The argument

Nelson: overcoming isometrics — pull as hard as possible against something immovable, 3-5 s, measured every rep — improve force output that shows up in climbing. Yielding isometrics like heavy hangs improve connective tissue stiffness but transfer less at higher levels.

Evidence status: no randomised trial in climbers comparing overcoming to yielding on climbing performance. Plausible mechanism, supported indirectly by the general literature on maximal-intent contractions. Still a mechanism argument.

What is well supported: intent matters more than load for early-phase force, and measurement drives intent. Pulling at "hard" produces reliably less than pulling at "hard, and I can see the number."

## Implications

- Buy a load cell. A Tindeq turns recruitment work from guesswork into numbers I can chase, and unlocks [[Critical Force]] testing as a by-product. Without one [[Recruitment Pulls]] still work, minus the feedback that makes them work well.
- 3-5 s efforts. Longer is a strength-endurance set.
- Three minutes rest minimum. Nervous-system work; metabolite accumulation is the enemy.
- Track peak per rep. Stop when peak drops >5% from the session best. Two more reps past that trains fatigue tolerance.
- Immediately after warm-up, before anything else.

## Potentiation

Real and short-lived. Near-maximal efforts raise subsequent output for a window measured in minutes. On a project day this is why a hard warm-up boulder before the best attempt often helps, and why sitting in the shade for twenty minutes after it doesn't.
