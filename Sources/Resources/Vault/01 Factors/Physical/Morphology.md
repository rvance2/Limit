# Morphology

My proportions. Untrainable, and mostly not the problem I'd blame it for.

## The data

A 2023 systematic review: ape index doesn't differ significantly between sport climbers of various skill levels, and anthropometric variables including ape index and height don't appear important in climbing. One multiple regression put trainable variables — strength, endurance, experience — at 58.9% of performance variance and anthropometrics at 0.3%. That figure comes from a coaching organisation, so the precision is marketing and the direction is sound.

Lattice's n=901 anthropometric table is quietly informative. Mean arm span by group: 73 cm intermediate, 72.16 advanced, 70.38 elite, 68.29 higher elite. Mean height: 181.25, 176.82, 176.94, 174.48. Their best climbers were slightly *shorter* with slightly *less* span. Almost certainly a strength-to-weight artefact rather than evidence that short helps, but it's the opposite of the assumption.

Boulderers as a population do differ from non-climbers in mass, density, fat percentage, lean mass, arm span and leg length, biggest gaps in span and lean mass. Population differences, not performance differences.

## Reframe

Morphology doesn't set my ceiling. It sets which beta works. A 6-foot climber and a 5-foot-4 climber on the same V9 solve different problems with the same holds, and both problems have solutions. The failure is trying the tall-person beta at 5-foot-4 for eight sessions.

So morphology is a [[Beta]] input, not a training target. [[Beta Lab]] has the protocol: three sequences per crux minimum, one assuming I'm shorter than I am, one assuming I'm more flexible.

## What I can change

Not proportions. Two adjacent things:

- Lean-to-fat ratio, within limits that don't compromise health. Read [[Energy]] first. The RED-S literature is unambiguous about chronic under-fuelling, and climbing has a bad record here.
- [[Flexibility and Mobility]], which expands effective reach in specific positions without changing span.
