# Tension

Whether force from my feet reaches my fingers or dissipates somewhere in the middle. On steep rock it's the difference between a foothold that works and a foothold that's decoration.

## The data

Saeterbakken et al. (2018), the only randomised comparison. Nineteen advanced and elite climbers, ten weeks, twice weekly, isometric vs dynamic core training, matched for intensity, volume, rest.

Messier than the headline:

- Isometric: +10.8% trunk flexion MVC, +29.6% body-lift test. No change in body lock-off or rotation.
- Dynamic: +5.0% to +14.9% across core strength tests including both rotation directions. +33.8% body-lift, p=0.100, not significant at n=9 per arm.
- Neither beat the other on anything.
- Finger hang +4% both, not significant.

Two things. It violates training specificity in an interesting way — the isometric group improved on the *dynamic* climbing test and the dynamic group improved on the *isometric* strength tests. And baseline trunk flexion MVC was 94-99% of bodyweight, which the authors flagged as 30-50% above an age-matched non-climbing population from the same lab.

Climbers already have strong cores. Small room, small effects. Authors' recommendation: dynamic for oblique strength, isometric for transfer. I do both, one circuit, twice weekly, without pretending my grade is hiding there.

## What tension actually is

Core strength is a component, not the whole:

- Toe and foot strength to hold a smear under load.
- Hip flexor and adductor endurance to keep a foot on when it wants to cut.
- Shoulder and scapular stability to keep the pulling arm rigid. My weak link — [[Shoulder Capacity]].
- Timing. Tension is a coordination skill applied at the right instant, which is why it improves fastest on the wall.

## Programming

Twice weekly, 15 min, end of session. Nothing in the circuit is neurally demanding enough to need priority placement.
