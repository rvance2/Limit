# Flexibility and Mobility

Weakly predictive of grade, occasionally decisive for one move. Both true, and I'd normally only believe one.

## The data

Lattice, n=901, standing box split against a wall, heel-to-heel:

- Combined: R = 0.140, R² = 0.020. Significant at p<0.001 only because n is large. Explains 2%.
- Advanced: adj R² 0.072, p = 0.233. ns.
- Elite: R = 0.026, p = 0.624. Nothing.
- Higher Elite: p = 0.909. Nothing.

A commenter made the sharp point that heel-to-heel distance in centimetres partly measures leg length, so the test conflates flexibility with height. Normalising to stature would have been better. Take 2% as an upper bound.

Against that: elite climbers scored significantly better than intermediates and non-climbers on an adapted Grant foot-raise test, which measures hip flexion range. So elite climbers *are* more mobile. They're also stronger, lighter and more experienced, and the mobility may be a consequence of years of high stepping.

## Where it decides things

Not in aggregate. In the specific.

- A high step I can't reach is a beta I can't use. Sometimes the only beta.
- A drop knee needs hip internal and external rotation. Limited range means either I don't do it or my medial knee absorbs what the hip should. The second is how climbers wreck knees.
- Ankle dorsiflexion decides whether my hips get over a high foot.
- Shoulder range decides whether a wide gaston is available, and for me also decides risk. [[Shoulder Capacity]].

## Programming

Twelve minutes, 3×/week, [[Hip and Ankle Mobility]]. Constant across all 22 weeks — mobility is maintained, not periodised, and losing it costs beta options.

No more than that. The 2% isn't moving and the hours are better spent on [[Finger Strength]] or [[Footwork]]. The reason to do it at all is keeping the option space open for the day the project demands something weird.
