# Motor Learning

How to structure practice so the skill is still there in nine weeks. Most transferable research in the vault, none of it climbing-specific.

## Contextual interference

Replicated widely, confirmed in a 2024 systematic review and meta-analysis: random practice schedules produce worse performance during acquisition and better performance on retention and transfer tests than blocked schedules.

Blocked = practise A repeatedly, then B, then C. Random = interleave unpredictably. Random feels worse. Works better.

Two proposed mechanisms. Elaboration: switching forces inter-item comparison, enriching encoding. Forgetting-reconstruction: switching forces me to discard and rebuild the motor plan each trial, and the rebuilding is the learning.

The 2025 methodological review is more cautious about generalisability than the enthusiastic version suggests. Well-supported, not settled.

## What it changes

**Skill sessions random.** Not six laps on the same slab. Slab, overhang, compression, slab again, unpredictably. [[Style Roulette]].

**Project sessions blocked.** Working a crux is deliberately low-interference because I want acquisition speed, not transfer. One of the few places blocked is correct.

**Accept looking bad in skill sessions.** If I feel more competent at the end than the start, the session was too easy or too blocked. Psychologically horrible, worth tolerating.

## Practice variability

Higher learning rates from variable practice than repetitive protocols. Applied: vary the constraints, not just the problems. Same boulder no left hand. Same boulder feet only on smears. Same boulder eyes closed for two moves.

## Imagery link

Motor imagery activates overlapping neural substrate with execution, which is the basis of PETTLEP. Practice I can do with wrecked skin. [[Visualization]].
