# Pacing and Rhythm

Where to move fast, where slow, where to stop moving. Sounds trivial on four moves. It's frequently the difference between the attempt that works and the four that didn't.

## What the research supports

Two findings, both slightly counterintuitive.

More experienced climbers spend a *greater* percentage of time immobile than less experienced ones. The authors read it as co-adaptation — stillness preserves stability, particularly when the priority is keeping the centre of mass close to the wall. Stillness is a skill, not hesitation.

Jerk coefficient of the hip trajectory — third derivative of position, so rate of change of acceleration — is lower in more experienced climbers. Less jerk means fewer unnecessary accelerations and decelerations, which means less peripheral forearm fatigue. [[Movement Efficiency]].

Together: good pacing isn't uniformly fast or slow. Fast where speed cuts time-under-tension on a bad hold, still where stillness buys position, smooth in transitions.

## My two errors

**Rushing the setup.** Arriving at the crux with hips slightly wrong because I moved through the preceding move too fast. The crux then feels impossible and I conclude I'm weak. Almost always a setup problem.

**Hesitating at the commitment point.** Pausing where the position costs more to hold than the move would cost to do. [[Commitment]]. On a bad crimp, thinking about it is the most expensive option available.

## Training

- Film every project attempt. Look for where I decelerate. Mid-move deceleration is nearly always wasted.
- Rehearse tempo, not just sequence. Count it out loud — one, two-three, four. Then match the count on the wall.
- On a boulder I've done, climb it at three tempos: fast as possible, slow as possible, natural. Slow teaches which positions are actually stable and which I was rushing past. Fast teaches which holds I don't need to fully engage.
