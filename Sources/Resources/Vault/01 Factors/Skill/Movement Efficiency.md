# Movement Efficiency

The measurable version of climbing well. Two metrics in the literature, both requiring me to think about my hips rather than my hands.

## Geometric entropy

Quantifies path complexity of the centre of mass during an ascent. Lower = more direct, more economical. It changes with repeated ascents of the same route, which makes it an index of learning — first ascent high entropy, fifth lower.

Field version: watch my hip path on video. Wandering, looping, or travelling further than the vertical distance gained means I'm spending energy on transport rather than progress.

## Jerk

Third time derivative of position. Total jerk of the hip trajectory is lower in more experienced, more fluent climbers, and the jerk coefficient has been proposed as a fluency indicator because it captures spatial and temporal aspects in one number.

Field version: count accelerations and decelerations inside a move. Every speed-up-slow-down within a single move is jerk, and it's nearly always a coordination failure rather than a necessity.

## What it means for four moves

Less than on forty, but in a specific way: reducing unnecessary acceleration lowers peripheral forearm fatigue and improves local oxygen availability. That's what I need across a twenty-attempt project session, not within a single attempt.

So the payoff isn't "I use less energy on the send." It's "attempt fifteen is as good as attempt three."

## Drills

- **Video and hip-trace.** Film side-on. Trace the hip path with a finger on the screen. Compare against someone who found the boulder easy.
- **One-move-one-breath.** Exactly one breath per move. Forces rhythm, exposes where I flail.
- **Minimum-moves.** Familiar boulder, fewest hand movements possible. Punishes readjustment.
- **Straight-arm audit.** Where am I bent-armed unnecessarily? A bent arm is a decision to spend biceps on a position skeleton could hold.
