# Focus

Where attention sits during the attempt. Applied research here is thinner than the general attentional-focus literature, so these are principles, not protocol.

## External beats internal

The motor-skills literature consistently finds external focus — on the effect of the movement, or the target — outperforms internal focus on body parts. "Push the foot into the hold" beats "contract your calf."

So my cues point at holds, directions and outcomes, not muscles. "Left hip to the wall" is a position cue and works. "Engage your core" is internal and mostly produces bracing without direction.

## One cue per attempt

An attempt lasts eight seconds. I get one cue. Chosen before pulling on, not revised mid-attempt.

Three things to fix means a beta problem or a rehearsal problem, not a focus problem. Fix them in slow-motion rehearsal, then take one cue into the full-speed attempt.

## A directive for every move, on a redpoint burn

The one-cue rule above is for learning a move. On a send attempt the problem inverts, and this is the sharper idea:

> "For the first move and the cross, I had enough micro beta to focus on mentally in order to distract from the macro idea of sending the boulder. It takes you out of the flow if you're thinking about the implications of your send. So you always want some directive for every move. For the last move, I just didn't have enough directive."

So the function of a movement cue during a redpoint isn't primarily to improve the movement. It's to **occupy the attention that would otherwise go to the outcome**. A move without a directive is a hole where outcome-thinking gets in — and outcome focus is the failure mode listed below.

He quantified it honestly: the cue was worth maybe 5% physically and 15% for directing his mind so he could do the move in link.

Reconciling the two: **one cue while learning the move, a directive for every move once linking.** Different jobs. If I can't name a directive for a move in my sequence, that's the move I'll blow on the good burn.

Source: [[Burden of Dreams — Wheeler and Shriber]]. Tier 3, but it maps cleanly onto the attentional-focus literature and onto my own failure pattern.

## Where attention goes when it fails

**Outcome focus.** Thinking about sending during the attempt. Reliably degrades performance. Fix: process cue, defined in advance.

**Self-monitoring.** Watching myself climb, checking whether it feels right. Fix: external cue, plus enough rehearsal that the movement doesn't need monitoring.

**Threat scanning.** Attention pulled to the fall, the pads, the audience. Fix: pre-attempt routine that resolves the threat before pulling on. Check the pads once, then stop checking.

## Pre-performance routine

Not mystical — a fixed routine removes decisions, which frees attention. Short and identical every time: chalk, breath, cue, pull on. Four beats. Over fifteen seconds and it's a ritual, not a routine.
