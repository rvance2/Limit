# Intent

The gap between trying and trying maximally. Bigger than I believe, and the cheapest thing here to improve.

## Evidence, such as it is

Direct climbing evidence: none I found. What supports it:

- Early-phase [[Rate of Force Development]] is dominated by neural drive rather than muscle size, and neural drive responds to instruction and feedback.
- Measurement changes output. Nelson's recruitment protocol works partly because I see the number every rep. Well-known effect in strength testing.
- The campus board researchers gave real-time biofeedback on a screen during maximal isometric pull-ups specifically to ensure maximal effort. They didn't trust "pull hard" as an instruction, and they were right not to.

That last point is the argument in miniature. If exercise scientists won't trust trained athletes to produce maximal force without a live readout, I shouldn't trust myself either.

## What sub-maximal intent costs

On [[Recruitment Pulls]] I can produce 90% of peak and it feels like 100%. Ten sessions at 90% is ten sessions at the wrong intensity for a nervous-system adaptation.

On a limit attempt it looks like: pulling on before I've decided to actually do the move, releasing a fraction early when it feels unlikely, letting the foot come off because I'd half-accepted the fall. All look like failed attempts. None are information about whether the move is possible.

## Training it

1. **Measure something every rep.** Load cell for recruitment work. Attempt-by-attempt notes on limit boulders: highest hold, and whether it was genuine.
2. **Name the attempt in advance.** Out loud or silently — "this is a real one" or "this is a rehearsal." Rehearsals are allowed and useful. Attempts that were secretly rehearsals aren't.
3. **Cut the session when intent goes.** Three consecutive non-genuine attempts ends it. [[Attempt Discipline]].
4. **Fewer attempts.** Six genuine beat twenty half-hearted, cost less skin, leave better data.

## The uncomfortable part

Maximum intent on a boulder I won't do produces repeated, obvious, total failure. Hampton's line is worth keeping: some of his most productive training seasons included not sending a single hard boulder in the gym. If I need to send to feel the session worked, I'll unconsciously choose easier boulders and the plan quietly becomes something else.
