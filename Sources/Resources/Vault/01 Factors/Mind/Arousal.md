# Arousal

Physiological and psychological activation. Too little and I can't produce force. Too much and I can't produce accuracy.

## The model

Hanin's Individual Zones of Optimal Functioning, developed over three decades of applied work. Central claim: the optimal activation level is individual. Some athletes perform best at high anxiety, some low, and the population-average inverted-U says nothing about any specific person.

A meta-analysis of 19 studies from 1978 to 1997 — 146 effect sizes, 6,387 participants — gave reasonably good support to the in-zone versus out-of-zone distinction. Real evidence base, moderate effect sizes, recall-based measurement with known problems.

Hanin's other useful move: helpful versus unhelpful, not positive versus negative. Anger can be functional. Calm can be dysfunctional. What matters is whether the state is in my zone for the task.

## Task-dependence

Where I'd get this wrong. Optimal arousal for a limit crux needing maximum force isn't optimal arousal for a technical slab needing precision. High arousal helps force production and degrades fine motor control.

Four-move boulder with a powerful crux: probably higher than I think. Boulder whose crux is a delicate foot on a smear: lower.

## Finding my zone

Not by feel — my memory of my best performances is unreliable. Log it. [[Arousal Calibration]] has the protocol: rate activation 1-10 before every meaningful attempt, log the outcome, and after 30-40 data points see where the successes cluster.

Common pattern in boulderers is that best attempts sit at 6-7, not 9-10, because 9-10 costs precision. That's a pattern, not my number.

## Manipulating it

Up: music, short explosive movement, breath-holds, competitive framing, a hard warm-up boulder immediately before. Potentiation from a near-maximal effort also raises output for a few minutes. [[Recruitment]].

Down: slow nasal breathing with a long exhale, deliberately slowing movement between attempts, extending the rest, shoes off.
